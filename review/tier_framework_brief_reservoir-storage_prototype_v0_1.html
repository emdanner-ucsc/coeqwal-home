Tier framework brief

How reservoir storage is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the reservoir storage indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: reservoir storage. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for reservoir storage are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

Reservoirs in California fill through the winter and spring, when most precipitation and runoff arrive, and draw down through the summer and fall as water is released for farms, cities, and river flows. How full a reservoir is at the end of April — just before the dry season begins — indicates the water supply available for the year ahead.

The indicator is not a single storage number. It is a measure of how often a reservoir is full and how often it is nearly empty at the end of April, across the 100 years each CalSim 3 scenario simulates. Frequency is used rather than an average because averages hide the extremes: a reservoir that is completely full half the time and completely empty the other half has the same average storage as one that sits steadily at half full, and the two conditions mean very different things to the people downstream.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed for eight reservoirs, selected from the many CalSim 3 represents to span a range of hydrologic settings, management purposes, operating agencies, and positions in the conveyance network. Together they account for a substantial fraction of Central Valley Project and State Water Project storage. San Luis Reservoir is jointly operated, and its state and federal shares are treated as separate locations.

What it is measured against

"Full" and "nearly empty" are defined for each reservoir from that reservoir's own observed record, not from its physical capacity. The California Data Exchange Center's end-of-April storage observations for 1990–2024 supply the reference: the 50th percentile of that 35-year record is the reservoir's high threshold, and the 20th percentile is its low threshold. Every reservoir therefore has its own pair of storage volumes, and every reservoir is compared to its own recent history rather than to the other seven.

Capacity is not used as the reference because many reservoirs carry flood-control requirements that hold spring storage below the physical maximum by design; measured against capacity, a reservoir operating exactly as intended would read as short. Comparing to typically observed storage avoids this, and follows the convention used in published reservoir condition summaries.

Using the median as the threshold for "high" may read oddly. It reflects the shape of the distribution: spring storage in most of these reservoirs sits near the operational maximum in most years, so the distribution is strongly skewed and the median falls at a relatively full condition. The 20th percentile approximately corresponds to storage levels reached during recent droughts.

How the indicator is computed

Each CalSim 3 scenario produces one end-of-April storage value per year for each reservoir — 100 values. Two fractions are taken from those 100 values:

  • the fraction of years at or above the high threshold, written frac_top;
  • the fraction of years at or below the low threshold, written frac_bot.

These are combined into a single number, a distance D:

D = (1 − frac_top) + frac_bot

D measures how far a scenario sits from the endpoint of the scale — a reservoir at or above its high threshold every year and never at or below its low threshold, giving D = 0. At the other end, a reservoir below its low threshold every year and never above its high threshold gives D = 2.0. Every scenario at every reservoir falls somewhere on that 0-to-2 range.

The two fractions carry equal weight in D. This is a deliberate choice in the construction of the indicator: frequent high storage does not offset frequent low storage. At some reservoirs low storage may matter disproportionately, and a weighted variant would be defensible; the uniform formulation was kept so that a tier means the same thing at all eight locations.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

Tier boundaries are set as pairs of frequency conditions — a minimum share of years above the high threshold, and a maximum share of years below the low threshold — which convert directly into cutoffs on D. A scenario's tier is the first row of the table below whose cutoff its D value does not exceed.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For reservoir storage the continuous value is the scenario's progress across its tier's interval on D, expressed as a fraction from 0 to 1 and added to the tier number. A value near the bottom of a tier's range sits close to the boundary with the tier above it; a value near the top sits close to the boundary with the tier below. Taking the whole-number part always returns the discrete tier.

The four intervals are 0 to 0.70, 0.70 to 0.92, 0.92 to 1.08, and 1.08 to 2.0. They are not equal widths, so a step of 0.1 in continuous value corresponds to a different change in D in Tier 2 than in Tier 4.

Worked from Figure 1: D = 0.50 sits in the Tier 1 interval, which runs from 0 to 0.70. Progress is 0.50 / 0.70 = 0.71, so the continuous tier value is 1 + 0.71 = 1.71.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a reservoir storage tier

A tier value near 1 describes a reservoir that, compared with its own record over 1990–2024, is at a full April condition more often and at a low April condition less often. Values through Tier 1 and Tier 2 broadly bracket recent observed performance: the low end of Tier 1 describes conditions well above the recent record, and the high end of Tier 2 describes conditions somewhat below it. Tier 3 and Tier 4 describe conditions that differ substantially from the recent record; at Tier 4 the reservoir is below its low threshold more often than it is above its high threshold.

What a reservoir storage tier does not resolve

The indicator is a frequency over 100 years at a single point in the annual cycle. It says nothing about when in the 100 years the low-storage years fall, so a scenario with its low years scattered and a scenario with the same low years arriving consecutively receive the same tier. It says nothing about storage in any other month. And because the eight reservoirs are treated independently, the tiers do not describe how much water the system holds in total.

The comparison is to observed history, which means it inherits any mismatch between the model and the record. At Millerton Reservoir the mismatch is large enough to matter: simulated April storage runs roughly 50 TAF below the observed record, while the spread of values is similar in both. The method is applied to Millerton unchanged, for consistency across the eight locations, but Millerton's tier values reflect that model-to-observation difference as well as the effect of the scenario being modeled, and should be read with that in mind.

Finally, the eight reservoirs are a selection. A reservoir important to a particular community may not be among them, and no tier reported here speaks to it.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to reservoir storage: the thresholds that define "full" and "low" are set at the 50th and 20th percentiles of a 35-year observed record, and both the percentiles and the record length are choices. Other defensible choices — a storage level tied to a particular delivery allocation, to temperature control capability, or to a longer record — would move the thresholds and therefore the tiers. The equal weighting of high-storage and low-storage frequency is likewise a choice in the construction. Tiers are comparable across scenarios for a given reservoir, and comparable in meaning across reservoirs, but they are not statements about volumes of water.
