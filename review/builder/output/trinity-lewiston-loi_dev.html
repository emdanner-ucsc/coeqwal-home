Environmental flows

Modeled tier outcomes at Trinity River at Lewiston

1. Headline

At Trinity River at Lewiston, modeled tier values across the 115 COEQWAL scenarios range from Tier 1 to Tier 4. Of the 92 scenarios run under the projected climate-stress hydroclimates, 76 (83%) keep the same tier label their strategy has under the historical hydroclimate and 16 (17%) shift to a different tier. Because the reported scenarios vary both the operating strategy and the hydroclimate, differences between scenarios combine climate and operations effects. These effects are addressed in section 4.

2. What this is

COEQWAL uses CalSim 3 — the state's main water-system model — to test how California's water system performs under different conditions. The model outputs are in the form of scenarios: each scenario is a pairing of a water management strategy (a way of operating the system, like today's rules or a proposed alternative) and a hydroclimate (patterns of temperature, rainfall, and snow that determine how much water is available over time). These results are sorted into four levels, called tiers: Optimal, Acceptable, At-risk, and Critical. Tiers describe what the model produces; they don't tell anyone what to do about it.

The model also tracks specific places in the study domain, which COEQWAL terms Locations of Interest, or LOIs. Cities, reservoirs, river reaches, farm regions, groundwater basins, and stretches of the Delta are each tracked individually. Every LOI sits inside one of nine area-level summaries COEQWAL uses to organize results (community water, agriculture, environmental flows, the Delta, salmon, in-Delta freshwater, Delta exports, reservoir storage, and groundwater).

This brief reports modeled tier values for one LOI — Trinity River at Lewiston (environmental flows area) across 115 scenarios from the COEQWAL library. It consists of 23 water management strategies paired with 5 hydroclimates, and is the same set used for every LOI brief.

4. Operations and climate

Because every scenario pairs a strategy with a hydroclimate, a difference in tier value between two scenarios can come from two places: changes in the strategy used to run the system, or changes in the hydroclimate the system is run under. At an LOI like Trinity River at Lewiston, both axes are reported, so the difference between scenarios can be separated into a strategy part and a hydroclimate part.

Holding the hydroclimate fixed at the historical hydroclimate, the spread among operating strategies at Trinity River at Lewiston is the strategy effect: 16 of the 23 strategies sit at Tier 4, while 5 sit at Tier 3, while 2 sit at Tier 1. Holding the strategy fixed at current operations, the change across hydroclimates is the climate effect: the tier value moves from 3.27 (Tier 3) under historical hydroclimate to 4.45 (Tier 4) under Moderate climate stress, 3.27 (Tier 3) under Moderate-high climate stress, 3.36 (Tier 3) under High climate stress, 4.54 (Tier 4) under Extreme climate stress. The remaining cells in the matrix combine both effects; Figure 2 separates them for every scenario.

Figure 2 shows this decomposition for every scenario. Each dot is one modeled strategy under one hydroclimate; current operations is gold. Within each column, strategies keep a fixed left-to-right order, so a strategy occupies the same relative position in every column and can be followed across the 5 hydroclimates.

6. About this place

This point sits on the Trinity River immediately below Lewiston Dam, in Trinity County in northwestern California. Lewiston Dam is where the Central Valley Project splits the Trinity's water: releases continue down the Trinity River toward the Klamath, while diversions pass through tunnels into the Sacramento River basin for CVP use. Flows below the dam are managed under the Trinity River Restoration Program, whose schedules vary by water-year type to support salmon and steelhead recovery.

8. What this doesn't say

This brief reports the tier results COEQWAL has generated based on what the CalSim 3 model produces under specific assumptions for one place. It doesn't predict what will happen at Trinity River at Lewiston, provide judgments about the modeled tier values, or recommend any course of action. The tier names (Optimal, Acceptable, At-risk, Critical) describe modeled conditions; what they mean for any particular community, decision, or person is not something the model itself decides.

The tier values shown reflect 115 scenarios from the COEQWAL library — pairings of 23 strategies with 5 hydroclimates — at this single LOI. They don't span every possible scenario, don't quantify uncertainty around the modeled values, and don't characterize other places. Other LOIs in the environmental flows sector, in different parts of the system, may show different patterns; see the area summary in the theme briefs and the explorer for the full LOI set. This remains a dev build: the values come from the 2026-07-02 locked dataset, and the project's final verification pass is still pending.

How to read these numbers

The tier scale runs from 1 to 4.99. Tier 1 is the favourable end of the scale: the tier framework names Tier 1 Optimal and Tier 4 Critical, so a smaller value sits nearer Optimal and a larger value nearer Critical. The API serves this number in a field named weighted_score; that name describes how the number is computed, and does not mean that a larger number is a more favourable result.

Two numbers describe each result. The integer tier_level is the tier label. tier_continuous is the position within the band of that label, and always falls inside it: tier_continuous is at least tier_level and less than tier_level plus one. Labels, counts and prose use tier_level; figure positions and statistics use tier_continuous.

Tier 1 is Optimal, Tier 2 is Acceptable, Tier 3 is At-risk, and Tier 4 is Critical. These are definitional category names supplied by the tier framework; what a category means for any reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

An area value is the unweighted mean over the locations in that area. Every location counts equally, regardless of the people, acres, or acre-feet it represents.

Any difference between two values under different hydroclimates contains both a climate effect and an operations effect; the two are separable only through the decomposition, because each is defined against its own reference. A strategy is compared with current operations under the same hydroclimate. Comparing one strategy with itself across hydroclimates is a different quantity: the climate effect plus the change in the operations effect.

Two thresholds do different jobs here. A difference smaller than 0.1 tier units is not reported as movement. Separately, where a count of locations is reported, a location is counted as differing from its comparison at 0.05 tier units; that floor exists to locate a difference so that locations can be counted, and a difference at it is not reported as a change in result.

The five hydroclimates are a purposive sample of climate stress levels. They are not a sequence, a trend, or a forecast, they carry no probabilities, and no mean is taken over them.

A run ID identifies one modeling run of one strategy under one hydroclimate. It is an internal identifier, used for citation and for tracing a value back to the data platform, not a name for the strategy; the public identity of a strategy is its descriptive name.
