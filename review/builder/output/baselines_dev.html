Baselines · Public Brief

Even with today's water management strategy unchanged, four projected hydroclimates move the modeled tier value away from its historical baseline in all nine areas of California's water system.

1. Headline

COEQWAL's water model paired today's California water management strategy with five hydroclimates: a historical baseline and four projected futures (Moderate climate stress, Moderate-high climate stress, High climate stress and Extreme climate stress). In all nine areas of the water system, the modeled tier value differs from its historical value under at least one projected hydroclimate, by 0.2 to 1.4 tier units. The largest movements are in environmental flows and freshwater for Delta exports. Eight of those areas move across a tier-band edge, so the tier label changes. One moves within a single band, so the label is the same under every hydroclimate while the value moves. Because the strategy doesn't change in this brief, what you're seeing is what the climate alone does to today's water system.

2. What this is

This is the results brief for the Understanding Today's Water System theme — the page where that theme's framing is turned into modeled outcomes. The theme explains why current operations is COEQWAL's reference point; this brief shows what the model produces when you hold today's operations fixed and vary the climate.

COEQWAL uses CalSim 3 — the state's main water-system model — to test how California's water system performs under different conditions. The model runs as scenarios: each scenario is a pairing of a water management strategy (a way of operating the system, like today's rules or a proposed alternative) and a hydroclimate (patterns of temperature, rainfall, and snow that determine how much water is available over time). To explore "what if today's rules continued under a different future?", you'd pair today's strategy with a projected hydroclimate. To explore "what if we changed how groundwater is managed?", you'd pair a different strategy with the same hydroclimate.

The model sorts outcomes into four levels, called tiers: Optimal, Acceptable, At-risk, and Critical. Tiers describe what the model produces; they don't tell anyone what to do about it.

This brief looks at one strategy — today's operating rules, kept in place — paired with five hydroclimates: a historical baseline and four projected futures (Moderate climate stress, Moderate-high climate stress, High climate stress and Extreme climate stress). That's five scenarios in total, all sharing the same strategy, so any difference between them comes from the hydroclimate, not from how the system is being run. Outcomes are reported for nine areas of the water system. No filter is applied.

3. What the model shows

The figure below shows the modeled tier value for each of the nine areas under each of the five hydroclimates. Values sit on a continuous scale from Tier 1 to Tier 4.99, with the four tier bands as shaded zones; the whole number below any value is its band. Each area carries a dashed level for today — current operations under the historical hydroclimate — and one lane per hydroclimate. The warm arrow in a lane runs from today to that hydroclimate's value, so its length is the hydroclimate contribution.

The blue mark that carries the strategy contribution in COEQWAL's other results briefs is a flat tick in every lane here. That is not a gap in the data: this brief holds the strategy fixed, so the strategy contribution is zero by construction (see §4).

4. Operations and climate

Because every scenario pairs a strategy with a hydroclimate, an outcome change between scenarios could in principle come from two places: changes in the strategy used to run the system, or changes in the hydroclimate the system is run under. COEQWAL's other briefs report scenarios where the strategy changes — and in those briefs, the model can separate an outcome change into a strategy part and a hydroclimate part.

In this brief, the strategy doesn't change. It's held constant by design across all five scenarios. So every outcome change reported here comes from the climate alone. Read alongside the other briefs, BASELINES is the climate-only reference: the part of the change the climate causes, separated out from anything operating rules can do.

5. The pattern across the system

Looking across all nine areas in Figure 1, nine move on the continuous tier scale between the historical hydroclimate and at least one projected hydroclimate, by 0.2 to 1.4 tier units. Among them, five change in a single direction reading across the figure's lanes, counting steps smaller than 0.1 tier units as flat. The remaining four move in more than one direction — environmental flows, Bay-Delta estuary ecology, winter-run Chinook salmon and groundwater storage do not move in a single direction across the projected hydroclimates.

How far a value moves and whether it changes tier band are two different measurements, and they do not track each other. Eight of the moving areas cross a band edge, so the tier label changes. One moves within one band: agriculture moves 0.6 tier units without the label changing. An area can move a long way inside one band, or a short way across an edge; the table under Figure 1 gives both readings for every area.

Because all the variation here comes from the climate (see §4), this is a reading of which areas are most sensitive to climate under today's strategy. It is not a reading of strategy tradeoffs — the cost of one management choice against another — because the strategy doesn't change here. The system's standing tradeoffs among competing uses are still present; what this brief shows is which of them climate stress sharpens, not what any operating decision adds or removes.

6. About specific places

Eight of the nine areas summarize many specific places across California — what the model calls Locations of Interest, or LOIs. Cities, reservoirs, river reaches, farm regions, groundwater basins, and stretches of the Delta are all tracked individually inside the sector summaries. Winter-run Chinook salmon is the exception — it's tracked as a single population, not as a summary over many places.

An area's tier averages across all the LOIs in that area. That means an area can look stable at the summary level while containing individual places under stress, or look stressed while containing places that are doing well. To see how a specific place is modeled — a particular community, river stretch, or basin — the data explorer lets you look at outcomes one LOI at a time. This brief stays at the summary level.

8. What this doesn't say

This brief reports what the model produces under specific assumptions. It doesn't predict what will happen, judge whether the outcomes are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier names (Optimal, Acceptable, At-risk, Critical) describe modeled conditions; what they mean for a particular community, decision, or person is not something the model itself decides.

The outcomes shown reflect today's water management strategy paired with the historical baseline and four modeled projected hydroclimates. They don't span every possible hydroclimate, don't include alternative ways of operating the system (those are in the other briefs), and don't go below the area-level summary. For specific places, see §6 and the explorer.

How to read these numbers

The tier scale runs from 1 to 4.99. Tier 1 is the favourable end of the scale: the tier framework names Tier 1 Optimal and Tier 4 Critical, so a smaller value sits nearer Optimal and a larger value nearer Critical. The API serves this number in a field named weighted_score; that name describes how the number is computed, and does not mean that a larger number is a more favourable result.

Two numbers describe each result. The integer tier_level is the tier label. tier_continuous is the position within the band of that label, and always falls inside it: tier_continuous is at least tier_level and less than tier_level plus one. Labels, counts and prose use tier_level; figure positions and statistics use tier_continuous.

Tier 1 is Optimal, Tier 2 is Acceptable, Tier 3 is At-risk, and Tier 4 is Critical. These are definitional category names supplied by the tier framework; what a category means for any reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

An area value is the unweighted mean over the locations in that area. Every location counts equally, regardless of the people, acres, or acre-feet it represents.

Any difference between two values under different hydroclimates contains both a hydroclimate contribution and a strategy contribution; the two are separable only through the decomposition, because each is defined against its own reference. A strategy is compared with current operations under the same hydroclimate. Comparing one strategy with itself across hydroclimates is a different quantity: the hydroclimate contribution plus the change in the strategy contribution.

Two thresholds do different jobs here. A difference smaller than 0.1 tier units is not reported as movement. Separately, where a count of locations is reported, a location is counted as differing from its comparison at 0.05 tier units; that floor exists to locate a difference so that locations can be counted, and a difference at it is not reported as a change in result.

The five hydroclimates are a purposive sample of climate stress levels. They are not a sequence, a trend, or a forecast, they carry no probabilities, and no mean is taken over them.

A run ID identifies one modeling run of one strategy under one hydroclimate. It is an internal identifier, used for citation and for tracing a value back to the data platform, not a name for the strategy; the public identity of a strategy is its descriptive name.
