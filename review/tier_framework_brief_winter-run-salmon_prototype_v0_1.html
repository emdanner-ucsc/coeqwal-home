Tier framework brief

How winter-run Chinook salmon are measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the winter-run Chinook salmon indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: winter-run Chinook salmon. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for winter-run Chinook salmon are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

Sacramento River winter-run Chinook salmon are endemic to the Sacramento River. The run declined through the twentieth century with habitat loss, harvest, and the reconfiguration of California's water infrastructure, reaching a critical point in the late 1980s; it was listed as endangered by the state in 1989, as threatened federally the same year, and uplisted to endangered in 1994. It remains in danger of extinction. Because winter-run spawn in summer and depend on releases of cool water from Shasta Dam for their eggs to survive, they sit at the center of Sacramento River water operations.

The indicator is the percentage of currently available spawning capacity filled by natural-origin female spawners. Spawner abundance is one of the standard measures of salmon population status, because a returning spawner has survived every stage of the life cycle; females are the focus because they drive reproduction and are the group whose numbers can be limited by available spawning habitat. Expressing abundance as a share of capacity rather than as a count makes it a measure of how much of the available habitat the population is using.

Natural-origin fish — those from eggs laid in the river rather than raised in a hatchery — are counted, because they spend their entire juvenile lives in the river system and therefore respond to changes in water allocation in ways hatchery-reared fish do not. The population model does account for hatchery-origin fish whose offspring, spawned in the river, become part of the natural-origin population in later generations.

Unlike every other COEQWAL sector, this indicator is not computed from CalSim 3 output directly. CalSim 3 flows and temperatures are inputs to a separate population model, and the indicator is computed from that model's output.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed for one population at one location: winter-run Chinook salmon in the Sacramento River. In scenarios that include reintroduction of spawners to the McCloud River, fish ultimately bound for the McCloud are counted in the Sacramento River before they move upstream, so the single location holds throughout.

Scenarios were run both with and without reintroduction. A selected subset of the reintroduction runs is carried in the visualization tools, identified by a trailing -R on the scenario number; results for all runs, with and without, are available for download.

What it is measured against

The reference is current spawning habitat capacity: 34,000 female spawners, the sum of capacity below Keswick Dam and in the McCloud River. The same figure is used for every scenario, and for runs with and without reintroduction, so that the percentage means the same thing in all of them and scenarios remain comparable.

The tier boundaries are anchored to two things. The Tier 3 and Tier 4 boundary at 6% of capacity corresponds to an escapement of about 3,000 spawners — converted to females using the observed average sex ratio of age-3 and age-4 spawners over 2003–2020 — which is the level below which the winter-run harvest control rule requires changes to commercial harvest practices. That is an external regulatory reference point rather than a modeling choice. The boundaries at 18% and 50% describe degrees of habitat saturation and were set by the sector authors.

How the indicator is computed

The population model is a spatially explicit state-space life cycle model. It tracks individuals by geographic area, life stage, and month through the freshwater phase, then annually once fish enter the ocean, and propagates survival forward across generations. Submodels represent egg survival, habitat capacity, river outmigration survival, and survival through the Delta; all of them are driven by flow, and for COEQWAL the flow comes from CalSim 3.

Three submodels were modified for this project. River outmigration was moved to a daily timestep, because monthly CalSim 3 volumes were disaggregated into daily hydrographs that carry seasonal structure — wet season baseflows, the snowmelt recession, and storm-driven peaks — and a monthly flow-survival relationship was too coarse to use them; daily survival is then scaled back to the model's monthly step, weighted by the proportion of fish beginning migration each day. Through-Delta survival is estimated by machine learning emulators of the enhanced particle tracking model, which reproduce its behavior from CalSim 3 flows at a small fraction of the computational cost, because the full model cannot run at the turnaround this project needs. A reintroduction submodel moves a portion of the Sacramento River spawners to the McCloud River, where cool spring-fed water year-round raises egg-to-fry survival; it accounts for adult collection, adult survival, juvenile collection, and juvenile survival, and was parameterized at a high quality of implementation.

From the model's output, the indicator is assembled in four steps.

Step 1 — convert abundance to a share of capacity

For each year, divide the number of natural-origin female spawners by the 34,000-female capacity.

Step 2 — smooth by generation

Take a three-year rolling average, winter-run generation time being about three years. This averages across the fluctuation of individual year classes within a generation.

Step 3 — take a conservative position on model uncertainty

The model is run 1,000 times per scenario, drawing from the distribution of uncertainty in its parameter estimates. The lower 20th percentile across those 1,000 traces is used for each year — a moderately risk-averse choice, so the reported value is one the model reaches or exceeds in 80% of its runs.

Step 4 — take the maximum over the century

The indicator is the highest value that the smoothed, 20th-percentile series reaches at any point in the 100 years. Salmon populations fluctuate with hydrology, and a population rebuilding from low abundance would not be expected to hold at its peak every year; a maximum asks whether a scenario allows the population to reach a given level of habitat use at least once, across all three dominant age-3 cohorts, rather than whether it sustains it.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For winter-run salmon the continuous value is the indicator's linear position between the two thresholds of its tier, measured from the tier's upper threshold downward, and added to the tier number. A continuous value of 3.00 sits at the top of Tier 3, closest to Tier 2; 3.99 sits at the bottom, closest to Tier 4.

Worked from the source document: an indicator of 13% falls in Tier 3, which runs from 18% down to 6%. It lies 42% of the way from the upper threshold toward the lower one, so the continuous value is 3.42.

Because the tier bands differ substantially in width, an equal step in continuous value corresponds to a very different change in habitat use depending on the tier: 0.1 of continuous value is 1.2 percentage points of capacity in Tier 3 and 3.2 in Tier 2.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a winter-run salmon tier

A tier value near 1 describes a population that, at its strongest point in the 100-year simulation, filled more than half the spawning habitat available to it. Higher tier values describe populations using progressively less of that habitat, down to Tier 4, where the population stays below the abundance at which harvest management changes are triggered.

Because the indicator is a maximum, it describes what a scenario permits at its best moment, not what it sustains. A scenario reaching Tier 2 did so in one three-year window somewhere in the century; it does not follow that the population held there.

What a winter-run salmon tier does not resolve

Which part of the life cycle a scenario supports. The tier reflects cumulative survival across every stage. Winter-run depend on the Shasta cold water pool for egg survival in summer, and a scenario that provides generous flows for outmigration but does not maintain that cold water pool — or provide access to the cool McCloud River — will not carry those flow benefits through to the tier, because the eggs did not survive to use them. Other Central Valley salmonids are less dependent on summer cold water releases and may respond differently to the same scenario.

Actions other than water allocation. Salmon recovery depends on habitat, harvest, and hatchery management as well as hydrology. Scenarios that change hydrology alone operate on one of those, and the tier is bounded accordingly.

The hydrologic record's particular sequence. The population is started at its contemporary size and run through the CalSim 3 1922–2021 hydrology, which opens with the 1930s drought. Modeled populations therefore decline at the start of every scenario, and much of what distinguishes scenarios is how far they slow that decline and how the population rebuilds afterward. A different sequence of wet and dry years, including droughts more severe than any in the record, would produce different results.

The reintroduction assumption. The reintroduction submodel assumes high-quality implementation, with small losses at collection, transport, and migration. Programs with lower implementation quality would not reach the same tiers; a more adaptive program, adjusting the timing and number of reintroduced spawners, could exceed them.

The hatchery floor. The conservation broodstock program at Livingston Stone National Fish Hatchery contributes a low-level floor to the natural-origin population, because hatchery-reared fish spend their rearing stages largely insulated from water management and their river-spawned offspring count as natural-origin. Some scenarios therefore stay at low abundance without the modeled population reaching zero.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to winter-run salmon: the indicator is the maximum of a smoothed, conservatively selected series, so it describes the best three-year window a scenario produced rather than typical or final conditions. The 34,000-female capacity is held fixed across all scenarios, including those with reintroduction, which makes the percentage comparable but means it does not register changes in how much habitat exists. The 20th-percentile selection is a deliberate risk posture, not a central estimate. And the tier reflects the output of a population model driven by CalSim 3, so it inherits the assumptions of both models.
