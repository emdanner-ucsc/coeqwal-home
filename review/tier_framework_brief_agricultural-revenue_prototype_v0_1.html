Tier framework brief

How agricultural revenue is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the agricultural revenue indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: agricultural revenue. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for agricultural revenue are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

The Central Valley produces more than 300 agricultural commodities across over 6.5 million acres. Farms there draw on surface water delivered through reservoirs, canals, and conveyance operated by local, state, and federal agencies, and on groundwater, which in many areas supplements surface supply and in some areas is the only source. How much water arrives, and how reliably, is one determinant of what can be grown and what it is worth — alongside crop mix, soils, climate, and the decisions growers make.

The indicator is the percentage change in average annual agricultural revenue in a modeled scenario relative to conditions today. Revenue rather than water volume is measured because the same shortage has very different consequences depending on what is grown and on how readily a grower can adapt: fallowing low-value acreage and losing a permanent orchard are not comparable in acre-feet, and are comparable in dollars.

Like winter-run salmon, this indicator is not computed from CalSim 3 output directly. CalSim 3 deliveries are converted into a measure of water shortage, and that shortage is fed to a separate agricultural production model, which estimates revenue. The tier is assigned from that model's output.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed for 134 locations, the agricultural demand units CalSim 3 uses to represent water demand: 73 in the Sacramento basin, 58 in the San Joaquin, and 3 in the Tulare basin. Each is a district or grouping of districts with its own supply portfolio, and each is identified in the source documentation with the water agencies it represents, its basin, and whether it holds surface water supplies, groundwater, or both.

Locations south of the San Joaquin hydrologic region — many Tulare basin users, and agencies in Southern California such as the Metropolitan Water District — are represented in CalSim 3 only as surface water diversion nodes. Their groundwater pumping and the rest of their supply portfolios are not modeled, so they cannot be evaluated the way a full demand unit can, and they are excluded from this sector.

Crop acreage for each location comes from the 2020 Statewide Crop Dataset developed by LandIQ.

What it is measured against

Two reference points are involved, at two different stages, and keeping them apart matters for reading the result.

For shortage, the reference is each location's own delivery history within the model. Full surface supply for a non-drought year is defined as the 75th percentile of that location's modeled surface deliveries; normal groundwater use is defined as the median of its pumping. The 75th percentile is used rather than the maximum, since the maximum reflects exceptionally wet years and would overstate what is typically available.

For revenue, the reference is conditions today: the tier is a percentage change against present-day agricultural revenue at that location, so a scenario is being compared with what the location currently earns rather than with any target or optimum.

For scenarios other than the baseline, expected deliveries are computed from the baseline scenario (current operations under the historical hydroclimate) as surface deliveries plus shortages in each year — the deliveries that would have occurred with no shortage. Alternative scenarios are then measured against that expected level.

How the indicator is computed

The chain runs from modeled deliveries to a shortage percentage to revenue.

Step 1 — annual deliveries

CalSim 3 provides monthly surface and groundwater deliveries for each location. These are summed to annual totals, which is the resolution the production model requires.

Step 2 — identify shortage

CalSim 3 does not itself report unmet agricultural demand. It assumes demands are met even in dry years, by increasing groundwater pumping when surface deliveries fall — so within the model, shortage never appears. Reconstructing it requires inferring it from the substitution.

A year is classified as a shortage year when both conditions hold: surface deliveries fall below the 75th-percentile benchmark, and groundwater pumping exceeds its median. The second condition is what separates a real shortage from a wet year in which demand was simply lower — deliveries can fall without any pumping increase, and that is a reduction in demand rather than a shortfall in supply.

The shortage is then expressed as a share of total demand:

shortage (%) = ΔSW / D  =  ΔSW / (SW + GW)

where ΔSW is the reduction in surface supply relative to the benchmark, SW and GW are surface and groundwater supply, and D is total demand, taken as the sum of the two. Dividing by total supply rather than by surface supply is what makes the measure sensitive to portfolio composition: the same cut in surface deliveries is a large shortage for a location that depends on surface water and a small one for a location that mostly pumps.

Scenarios with groundwater pumping restrictions are handled differently, because those restrictions are already built into the CalSim 3 operations and generate shortage directly. For those, total shortage is the sum of shortage from reduced surface deliveries and shortage from the pumping restriction, capped so that it cannot exceed what pumping would have been without the restriction — which prevents the same groundwater from being counted twice.

Step 3 — estimate revenue

The annual shortage percentages are inputs to OpenAg, an agricultural production model built on positive mathematical programming. Given land use, production costs, prices, yields, and applied water, it finds the profit-maximizing crop pattern under the water available. Each location is modeled as an independent subregion, so crop mix and revenue are estimated as a function of that location's water availability, year by year. Averaging across the 100 years gives the average annual revenue that the tier is assigned from.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

The tier is the band that the percentage change in average annual revenue falls in.

Optimal		revenue increases

Acceptable		revenue declines by less than 5%
Tier 3
At-risk		revenue declines by 5% to 20%
Tier 4
Critical		revenue declines by more than 20%

Note that the Tier 1 and Tier 2 boundary sits at zero change. A location whose revenue is unchanged from today sits at the top of Tier 2, not in Tier 1: Tier 1 in this sector requires an increase, which is a stricter reading of "optimal" than in sectors where Tier 1 describes conditions at or above a reference.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For agricultural revenue the continuous value is the linear position of the revenue change between the two bounds of its tier, added to the tier number:

continuous tier = tier + (x_upper − x) / (x_upper − x_lower)

where x is the percentage change in revenue for that location and x_upper and x_lower are the less severe and more severe bounds of the tier it falls in.

Worked from the source document: a location whose revenue declines 15% falls in Tier 3, which spans declines of 5% to 20%. It lies two thirds of the way from the less severe bound toward the more severe one, so the continuous value is 3.67 — toward the more severe end of the band.

Tier 4 is unbounded below: revenue can decline by any amount. Values are capped at 4.99, so locations with declines beyond the point that produces 4.99 are not distinguished from one another. In the baseline scenario only a small number of locations reach that cap.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a agricultural revenue tier

A tier value near 1 describes a location whose modeled average annual agricultural revenue is higher than today's. Increasing values describe progressively larger declines, through less than 5% at Tier 2, 5–20% at Tier 3, and more than 20% at Tier 4.

These are averages over 100 years, not the effect of any individual drought. The measure should be read as a long-term indicator of economic drought impact rather than a statement about what happens in a given year. Averaging is what lets a single number carry both how often shortages occur and how severe they are, without needing separate indicators for each.

What an agricultural revenue tier does not resolve

Actual unmet demand. The shortage this sector measures is a reduction in surface deliveries relative to total supply, inferred from groundwater substitution. It is not a record of demand that went unmet, and it is shaped strongly by supply portfolio. A location that pumps nearly all its water shows little modeled shortage, because a cut in surface deliveries barely moves its total — this was visible in the pre-SGMA period, where groundwater-dependent locations registered limited drought impact within this framework. A surface-dependent location with senior water rights may likewise show small shortages, because its deliveries were not cut much.

Any individual farm. Results are computed at the demand unit scale, which aggregates many growers and areas into one operational unit. Local differences in groundwater access, conveyance, and infrastructure are not resolved, and no result here describes a particular farm or grower.

Feedback between water operations and farm economics. The two models are coupled in one direction only: CalSim 3 output informs OpenAg, and nothing returns. Their assumptions differ as a result — crop water demand varies year to year with weather in CalSim 3, while OpenAg holds agricultural demand assumptions fixed across the simulation.

Decision-making under uncertainty. OpenAg optimizes with perfect foresight: it makes cropping decisions knowing the entire 100-year sequence in advance. Real growers do not know what the next year holds. The modeled economic results therefore represent a well-adapted case, and outcomes under realistic uncertainty would be less favorable.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to agricultural revenue: the tier boundaries at 0%, −5%, and −20% are chosen categories of economic impact, not thresholds derived from any observed break in how farms respond. The shortage definition rests on two percentile choices — the 75th for normal surface supply and the median for normal pumping — and treats every increase in groundwater pumping as evidence of shortage, which is a conservative reading, since some of that pumping may be sustainable. Revenue is modeled, not observed, by a production model coupled in one direction to CalSim 3 and optimizing with foresight no grower has.
