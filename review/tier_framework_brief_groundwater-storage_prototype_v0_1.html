Tier framework brief

How groundwater storage is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the groundwater storage indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: groundwater storage. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for groundwater storage are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

The aquifers under the Central Valley supply irrigation and municipal water alongside surface deliveries. When pumping exceeds recharge over long periods, the water stored underground declines, and with it the availability of groundwater to wells, the support groundwater gives to rivers, and in places the land surface itself. Groundwater and surface water are linked: surface deliveries can substitute for pumping and can recharge aquifers, and when surface deliveries are short — through drought or through a lack of conveyance — pumping increases to make up the difference.

The indicator is a long-term trend in groundwater storage, not a level. A linear regression is fitted to the modeled storage time series for each region, and the slope of that line — feet of stored water gained or lost per year — is the quantity the tier is built from. A trend is used rather than a storage level because groundwater behaves differently from surface reservoirs: a reservoir can be emptied and refilled within a year or two, while aquifer storage in much of the Central Valley changes over decades. Short-term fluctuations around a trend are not what the indicator is measuring.

The term groundwater storage here means the water held in an aquifer across a region, not the depth to water in any one well. The two are related — a regional decline is usually accompanied by falling water levels in wells — but they are not the same measurement, and the value reported is a volume expressed as an equivalent depth over the region's area, not a depth to groundwater.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed for 42 regions, the groundwater model subregions corresponding to CalSim 3's Water Budget Areas. Each Water Budget Area covers a portion of the Central Valley floor with broadly similar landscape, water source, and hydroclimatic character, and is built from smaller demand units that define specific water use and administrative areas. Twenty-nine are in the Sacramento basin, nine in the San Joaquin, two in the Delta and eastside streams, and one in the Tulare basin.

The coupled groundwater model represents three aquifer layers of varying thickness and depth. For this indicator, all three layers within a region's boundary are summed to a single total storage value.

What it is measured against

Groundwater is the one sector measured against another model run rather than against an observed record or a fixed standard. Two reference points define the tier boundaries.

The first is the baseline storage trend: the slope fitted to a CalSim 3 simulation using DWR's adjusted historical hydrology, run identifier s0002. A scenario whose regional trend is at least as positive as this reference is at the Tier 1 end of the scale. Note that this is not the COEQWAL baseline used elsewhere in the project (s0020, current operations under the historical hydroclimate); a separate run is used so that every scenario in the catalog, including the COEQWAL baseline itself, can be evaluated against something external to it.

The second is the severe decline threshold, set at −0.015 feet per year and applied identically in all 42 regions. It was chosen from the distribution of long-term decline rates in the reference run, as the point separating more common drawdown from more extreme conditions. Where that line falls is a modeler's judgment, and it determines how Tier 3 and Tier 4 divide.

Three assertions underlie the direction of the scale, and the framework states them as assertions rather than as findings. A sustained downward trend reflects a persistent imbalance between use and replenishment. A stable or rising trend reflects use in balance with recharge. And a scenario whose decline is less severe than the reference has moved toward balance, which the scale is built to register even when the trend remains negative.

How the indicator is computed

Groundwater storage is written to a standard output file by every CalSim 3 simulation, as a monthly time series for each subregion. The computation runs in four steps.

Step 1 — one storage series per region

Sum storage across all three aquifer layers, then convert the volume to an equivalent depth by dividing by the region's surface area. A region holding 5 million acre-feet beneath 1 million acres has an equivalent depth of 5 feet.

Step 2 — restrict the period

The series is clipped to 1960–2021, discarding the first four decades of the simulation. Many regions show large, sharp storage declines in the opening years, which appear to be the groundwater model equilibrating from an initial condition that does not match its local starting state: after roughly the first twenty years the system responds in a fundamentally different way. Including those years would push regions that are stable or rising over the remainder of the simulation into Tier 3 or Tier 4 on the strength of an artifact.

Step 3 — fit a trend

Fit a linear regression to the clipped series. The slope, in feet per year, is the indicator.

Step 4 — compare against the two reference points

Compare the scenario slope to the baseline slope for the same region, and to the severe decline threshold.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

The tier is assigned by comparing the scenario's slope to the two reference points. The rows are evaluated in order, and the first whose condition is satisfied is the tier.

TierCondition on the fitted slopeWhat it describes
Tier 1
Optimal		slope ≥ the baseline slope for that regionstorage trend at least as strong as the reference run
Tier 2
Acceptable		slope ≥ 0, but below the baseline slopestorage stable or rising, but less so than the reference
Tier 3
At-risk		slope < 0, but above −0.015 ft/yrmoderate long-term decline
Tier 4
Critical		slope ≤ −0.015 ft/yrdecline beyond the severe threshold

Because the Tier 1 and Tier 2 boundary is the region's own baseline slope, it sits at a different value in every region. The Tier 3 and Tier 4 boundary does not: the severe decline threshold is the same everywhere. A tier therefore mixes a locally-referenced comparison at the top of the scale with an absolute one at the bottom.

The reference run itself is assigned Tier 0 and dropped from the reported results.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For groundwater storage the continuous value is a ratio of slopes, not a position along an interval of fixed width. Each tier has its own expression, and each is built so the result stays inside that tier's band:

TierContinuous valueWhat the ratio measures
Tier 11 + (baseline_slope / slope), when the baseline slope is positivehow far the scenario's rise exceeds the reference
Tier 11 + |baseline_slope| / (|baseline_slope| − baseline_slope + slope), when the baseline slope is negativethe same, where the reference is itself declining
Tier 22 + (1 − slope / baseline_slope)distance from the reference down to a flat trend
Tier 33 + (slope / severe_decline_threshold)distance from a flat trend toward the severe threshold
Tier 4min(4.999, 4 + (1 − severe_decline_threshold / slope))how far decline runs beyond the severe threshold

Two consequences follow, and they distinguish this sector from those whose continuous value is a linear position within a band.

The value is not linear within a tier. A continuous value of 1.58 can be read as roughly midway through Tier 1, but it is not 58% of the way from Tier 1 to Tier 2 in any measurable sense: the strongest Tier 1 condition is approached asymptotically rather than reached, so the interval has no far end to measure against.

Tier 4 is unbounded and is clipped. Decline can continue past any value, so the expression is capped at 4.999.

The formulation also admits a documented edge case. Where the baseline slope is negative and the scenario slope is positive — not a rare combination — the tier is 1 and the second Tier 1 expression applies. If the two slopes are exactly symmetrical about zero, for instance a baseline of −0.01 and a scenario of +0.01, the result is 1.33 regardless of how positive the scenario slope is. This did not arise in the more than 140 scenarios analyzed, but it is a property of the expression rather than an accident of the data.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a groundwater storage tier

A tier value near 1 describes a region whose modeled groundwater storage is rising at least as fast as under the reference run. Tier 2 describes storage that is stable or rising, but less strongly than the reference. Tier 3 describes a moderate long-term decline, and Tier 4 a decline steeper than −0.015 feet per year. Because the values within a tier come from slope ratios, they order regions and scenarios within a tier but do not measure a fixed distance.

What a groundwater storage tier does not resolve

Local conditions. The indicator averages storage across a whole subregion. Where pumping is clustered or uneven within a large region, areas in meaningful decline can be offset by areas that are stable or rising, and the tier will be less severe than local conditions warrant. The underlying groundwater model is itself coarse and approximates local water levels rather than resolving them.

Particular periods, and short intervals. The trend is fitted over 1960–2021 and identifies the long-run balance of recharge and pumping. It carries no information about variation driven by individual wet or dry sequences, and it says nothing about the four decades excluded from the fit.

Actual water levels. A tier summarizes a numerical model, and the model may not match measured conditions everywhere. The framework was designed to reduce the effect of such mismatches by referencing scenarios against another run of the same model rather than against absolute levels, but a tier does not translate to a water table depth at a location, or to a number of wells that would go dry.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to groundwater storage: the severe decline threshold at −0.015 feet per year is a judgment about where moderate decline becomes severe, applied uniformly across the Central Valley; a different threshold would redistribute Tier 3 and Tier 4 without any modeled quantity changing. The 1960 start of the trend period is likewise a choice, made to exclude what appears to be model equilibration. And because the Tier 1 and Tier 2 boundary is each region's own baseline trend, a Tier 1 result means the scenario's trend is at least as strong as a reference that may itself be declining — it is not a statement that groundwater is being replenished.
