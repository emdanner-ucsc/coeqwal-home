Tier framework brief

How environmental flows are measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the environmental flows indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: environmental flows. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for environmental flows are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

Flow is the master variable of a river ecosystem. The volume, timing, and variability of water moving down a channel drive the physical, chemical, and biological processes that a river depends on — scouring gravels, wetting floodplains, cueing migration, sustaining pools through the dry season. California rivers are highly variable by nature, and the ecological functions they support depend on that variability rather than on a steady quantity of water.

The functional flows approach describes this with five seasonal components of a year's hydrograph: the fall pulse, the wet season peak and baseflow, the spring recession, and the dry season baseflow. Each component can be characterized by metrics of magnitude, timing, frequency, duration, and rate of change. Every river has its own signature of these components, set by its watershed area, precipitation, the balance of rain and snowmelt, and groundwater influence, and the signature differs from year to year with hydrologic conditions.

The indicator asks, for each river and each modeled scenario, in how many of the 100 simulated years the modeled flows met the functional flow targets set for that river in that year, and separately, in how many years the river kept its natural seasonal pattern of higher flows in winter and spring than in the dry season. It is a count of years, not a volume of water.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed at 17 locations across the major Central Valley river basins, each corresponding to a CalSim 3 node where monthly river flow is estimated. Eight are on the Sacramento or San Joaquin mainstems and nine on major tributaries. They were chosen to span hydrologically distinct regions.

What it is measured against

Modeled flows are compared against functional flow targets built from each river's unimpaired flow — what the river would carry without dams and diversions — rather than against its observed regulated flow or against a fixed minimum. The targets are year-specific: a dry year carries a different target than a wet one, because holding a river to a wet-year target in a dry year would ask for water the river never had.

Building them took four steps. Daily reconstructed full natural flow records were passed through the functional flows calculator, producing annual values for every functional flow metric. Annual flow volumes, in both the reconstructed record and the CalSim 3 unimpaired series, were classified into water year percentiles using a log-normal fit to the historical record. Empirical relationships between water year percentile and each metric were then fitted by linear regression, so a metric value could be predicted for any year of the 100-year simulation; where a linear fit was poor, the metric was assigned by another rule. Finally, the annual metric values were assembled into continuous daily target hydrographs, one for each of the 100 years. Peak flow targets were capped at each river's reported channel capacity.

How the indicator is computed

The targets are daily; CalSim 3 reports monthly. Reconciling the two is the central operation, and it is done by converting both to seasonal volumes.

Step 1 — assign months to seasons

The wet season begins in the month containing the wet season start date and ends in the month before the spring peak. The spring season runs from the month containing the spring peak to the month before the dry season start. The dry season runs from the month containing the dry season start to the month before the next water year's wet season begins. Season lengths therefore differ from year to year and river to river.

Step 2 — convert targets to mean monthly seasonal flows

The daily target hydrograph is aggregated into monthly and then seasonal volumes, and each seasonal volume is divided by the number of months in that season. Normalizing by season length is what makes years with different season lengths comparable.

Step 3 — do the same to the modeled flows, and compare

Modeled monthly flows are aggregated over the same season boundaries and compared against the seasonal targets, year by year, for all 100 years of each scenario. A magnitude criterion is satisfied if the modeled seasonal volume reaches at least 95% of the target: a 5% buffer, applied to every seasonal magnitude target, in acknowledgement of uncertainty in the modeled flows.

Step 4 — check seasonality separately

Independently of magnitude, each year is checked for its seasonal pattern: whether mean monthly flow across the wet and spring seasons exceeds mean monthly dry season flow. A year in which it does not has an inverted seasonal pattern, whatever its volumes.

The result is two counts per river per scenario: the number of years meeting the magnitude criteria at each level of stringency, and the number of years preserving seasonality.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

Tiers are assigned from those counts. Every tier requires the seasonality condition in at least 95 of 100 years; the tiers differ in which magnitude targets must be met, and in how many years.

Tier 3

Tier 4

The 5% buffer applies to every magnitude target in the table. Note what distinguishes the tiers: Tier 1 asks for the full functional flow regime in nearly every year; Tier 2 relaxes the targets to dry-year values but still asks for peak flows; Tier 3 drops the peak flow requirement and asks only for baseflows. The seasonality condition is identical at all three, so a river with an inverted seasonal pattern in more than five years falls to Tier 4 regardless of how much water it carries.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For environmental flows the continuous value is set by how many years beyond the minimum a river satisfied the magnitude criteria for its tier. The tier's required count defines how many years could have qualified, and the value moves across that range:

continuous tier = tier + (100 − years satisfied) / (count of qualifying years)

where the count of qualifying years is the number of year-counts that would still place the river in that tier. For Tier 1, which requires 95 years, a river could satisfy the criteria in 95, 96, 97, 98, 99, or 100 years — six possibilities. For Tier 2 and Tier 3, which require 90, there are eleven.

Two worked examples from the source document. A river satisfying the Tier 1 magnitude criteria in 95 years, and the seasonality criterion in 95: 1 + (100 − 95) / 6 = 1.83. A river satisfying the Tier 2 criteria in all 100 years, and seasonality in 95, but failing the Tier 1 magnitude criteria: 2 + (100 − 100) / 11 = 2.00.

Tier 4 uses 90 in place of 100 in the numerator, since it has no required count of its own.

One property follows from the arithmetic and is worth stating plainly: because the number of qualifying years is small, the continuous value moves in coarse steps and does not reach the top of its band. Within Tier 1 it can only take the six values 1.00, 1.17, 1.33, 1.50, 1.67, and 1.83; within Tier 2 and Tier 3, eleven values from x.00 to x.91. The continuous value in this sector is a count expressed on a continuous scale, not a smoothly varying quantity.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a environmental flows tier

A tier value near 1 describes a river whose modeled flows meet the full range of functional flow targets in nearly every year, while keeping the natural seasonal pattern. Tier 2 describes a river meeting dry-year targets, including peak flows, in at least ninety years. Tier 3 describes a river meeting dry-year baseflow targets but not the peak flows that inundate floodplains and move sediment. Tier 4 describes a river meeting none of these, or one whose seasonal pattern is inverted in more than five years.

The framework is a comparison of modeled flows against defined flow objectives. It is not an ecological response model: a tier says how far modeled hydrologic conditions align with functional flow objectives, and does not quantify ecological health, species response, or habitat quality.

What an environmental flows tier does not resolve

Anything shorter than a season. CalSim 3 runs on a monthly timestep, and the comparison assumes that when a seasonal volume meets its target, the water within that season arrives distributed as the daily functional flow targets describe. That assumption cannot be checked with monthly data. Short-duration events — the peak flows that inundate floodplains, transport sediment, and move organisms — are precisely what a monthly timestep cannot resolve, and they are among the most ecologically consequential parts of the hydrograph.

The 17 locations are a sample of the network. A reach not among them is not described by any tier reported here.

The targets themselves are constructions. They are predicted from statistical relationships between water year percentile and metric values, fitted to reconstructed unimpaired records, and capped at reported channel capacities. They describe a defensible reconstruction of a river's natural functional flow regime, not a measurement of one.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to environmental flows: the tier thresholds — 95 years, 90 years, the 5% magnitude buffer, the choice of the 33rd and 10th water year percentiles as dry-year targets — are set by the framework's authors, and different values would redistribute the tiers without any modeled flow changing. Because every tier requires the same seasonality condition, the scale is more sensitive to seasonal inversion than to flow volume. And the whole comparison rests on monthly modeled flows standing in for daily ones, which is a limitation of the model rather than of the method.
