Tier framework brief

How freshwater for Delta exports is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the freshwater for Delta exports indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: freshwater for Delta exports. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for freshwater for Delta exports are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

The Delta is the hinge of California's water conveyance. Water stored in the comparatively wet Sacramento basin is moved through the Delta and pumped south to the San Joaquin and Tulare basins, the Central Coast, and Southern California. Two facilities in the south Delta do the pumping: the Jones plant, operated by the Central Valley Project, and the Banks plant, operated by the State Water Project.

The indicator is the total volume of fresh water exported through those two plants over the 100-year simulation, with volume discounted where the water pumped was salty. It is one number per scenario, and it is predominantly volumetric: salinity enters as a penalty on volume rather than as a measure in its own right.

Salinity is included this way for a specific reason. Delta operations have historically been managed to hold salinity at the pumps low, and the model reflects that — initial evaluation across a range of scenarios found that modeled salinity at the two plants very rarely exceeds the 900 µS/cm freshwater threshold. Salinity alone would therefore separate almost no scenarios. Using it to reduce the weight of the corresponding export volumes folds the information into a measure that does vary.

This sector overlaps with the community water systems and agricultural revenue sectors, which cover some of the same water, but measures something different. Those sectors evaluate what arrives at specific places, with the local context that makes a delivery sufficient or not. This one evaluates the volume and quality of water leaving the Delta, and covers a footprint that does not match theirs — both of those sectors include locations upstream of the pumps, which Delta exports do not serve at all.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed from data at two locations, the two south Delta export facilities, and combined into a single value per scenario.

The salinity variables are the maximum 14-day value within each month, not the monthly mean. They are the only salinity variables computed at the pumping plants in the CalSim 3 files underlying COEQWAL scenarios. Where salinity varies within a month, a 14-day maximum runs higher than a monthly mean, so this choice weights salinity somewhat more heavily than a mean-based measure would.

What it is measured against

Two reference standards are in play, and they come from different places.

For salinity, the same two thresholds used across the Delta sectors: 900 µS/cm, the boundary of recommended freshwater quality for all uses, and 2,500 µS/cm, the level no longer recommended for drinking water and at which irrigation begins to impair crops. Both derive from State Water Board drinking water benchmarks and from published agronomic response to salinity in irrigation water.

For volume, the reference is physical pumping capacity over the 100-year simulation. The three tier boundaries sit at approximately 61%, 56%, and 48% of the maximum physical capacity of the two plants across that period. Those percentages were chosen from a review of CalSim 3 baseline results and recent historical delivery amounts, with an allowance for variation across the more extreme hydroclimates. They were set so that current operations under the historical hydroclimate, including sea level rise, falls within Tier 2 — which means Tier 1 describes exports above the current-operations baseline and Tiers 3 and 4 below it.

How the indicator is computed

The computation runs over all 1,200 monthly timesteps at both facilities.

Step 1 — compute a salinity weight for each month at each plant

Water pumped at or below 900 µS/cm counts in full, with a weight of 1.0. Water above 2,500 µS/cm counts not at all, with a weight of 0. Between the two the weight falls linearly:

weight = (2500 − salinity) / (2500 − 900),  clipped to [0, 1]

Step 2 — discount each month's volume

Multiply the month's export volume by that month's weight.

Step 3 — accumulate

Sum the weighted monthly volumes across all 1,200 months at each plant, then add the two plants together. The result is a single salinity-adjusted export volume for the scenario, in millions of acre-feet.

Because salinity at the pumps rarely exceeds 900 µS/cm in the model, the adjusted total is close to the unadjusted total in most scenarios, and the tier is in practice driven by export volume. The salinity term distinguishes scenarios that achieve high export volumes by drawing saltier water at the pumps from those that achieve the same volumes without doing so.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For Delta exports the continuous value is the scenario's linear position between the volume boundaries of its tier, added to the tier number. A value near the bottom of a tier's range sits close to the higher-export boundary; a value near the top sits close to the lower-export boundary. A scenario 35% of the way from the Tier 2 boundary toward the Tier 3 boundary has a continuous value of 2.35, and reads as being halfway between Tier 2 and Tier 3 at 2.50.

Tiers 2 and 3 interpolate between their two stated volume thresholds. Tier 4 interpolates between the 400 MAF boundary and zero export.

Tier 1 is different, and the difference matters. Export volume has no theoretical upper bound, so there is no fixed anchor for the strongest Tier 1 condition. Instead the anchor is taken empirically: the highest salinity-adjusted export volume observed among all Tier 1 scenarios in the set being analyzed. A Tier 1 continuous value is therefore a rank within the ensemble, not a position on a fixed scale — adding a higher-export scenario to the set changes the anchor, and with it the continuous values of every other Tier 1 scenario. The discrete tier is unaffected.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a Delta exports tier

A tier value near 1 describes a scenario exporting a large cumulative volume of fresh water through the two south Delta plants over the century, without drawing salty water to do it. Increasing values describe lower export volumes, higher salinity at the pumps, or a combination. A continuous value of 2.5 is halfway between the Tier 2 and Tier 3 volume boundaries, and the discrete and continuous values are read the same way.

The tier boundaries were set so that current operations under the historical hydroclimate falls in Tier 2. A Tier 1 result therefore describes exports above that baseline, and Tiers 3 and 4 describe exports below it.

What a Delta exports tier does not resolve

Anything about timing. The indicator is a century-long total. South-of-Delta allocations are commonly reduced during droughts, which would show up as lower pumping and possibly higher salinity at the pumps in those particular years; if such reductions are infrequent or small relative to normal pumping, the cumulative total will barely register them. Detecting that pattern would require a different rule, built on variation or on volumes in specific periods.

The individual facilities. The two plants are summed. A scenario shifting volume between Banks and Jones does not move the tier.

Whether any particular user has enough water. A tier here does not imply shortage or surplus for any end user. Export volumes correlate with deliveries to farms and cities, but the community water systems and agricultural revenue sectors carry the local context — crop values, delivery sufficiency thresholds, alternative supplies — that determines what a delivery means where it lands. A volume-based measure does not.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to Delta exports: the volume boundaries were chosen from an inspection of recent delivery amounts and baseline model results, and are not tied to any particular consequence. If a consequence of interest were tied to a specific export volume or salinity level, the boundaries could be reset to it, and the tiers would change without any modeled quantity changing. The salinity term uses a 14-day maximum rather than a monthly mean, which weights salinity somewhat more heavily than a mean would. And a Tier 1 continuous value depends on which scenarios are in the analyzed set, so Tier 1 decimals are not comparable between analyses built on different scenario sets.
