Tier framework brief

How community water systems are measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the community water systems indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: community water systems. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for community water systems are reported in the results briefs.

This sector's source documentation carries three measures, and only one of them produces a tier. The tier-bearing indicator is community surface water reliability, and the body of this brief describes it. Two further measures — M&I supply shortage and welfare loss from supply shortage — are reported alongside it in the data explorer, cover a different set of locations, and are assigned no tiers. They are documented at the end of this brief, under Two additional measures, which produce no tiers.

What the indicator measures

The State Water Project supplies or recharges drinking water for some 27 million Californians, and the Central Valley Project for more than 6 million. Beyond household use, those deliveries keep schools, hospitals, and parks running. As climate changes the quantity and timing of supply, deliveries to community water systems and to the wholesalers that serve them may become less reliable.

The indicator measures how reliably modeled surface water deliveries cover the potable demand those deliveries currently serve. It combines two things that a single average would blur together:

  • Reliability — in how many of the 100 years deliveries substantially cover recent potable demand;
  • Severity — in the years they do not, how far short they fall.

Both are needed because a location short by a little in many years and a location short by a great deal in a few years are not in the same situation, and either can be masked by a mean.

The users counted are community water systems — under the Safe Drinking Water Act, systems serving 15 or more connections or 25 or more year-round residents, including cities, special districts, and investor-owned utilities — together with wholesale drinking water providers that sell to those systems without serving retail customers themselves.

Only direct potable use is counted. Deliveries used entirely for groundwater recharge or aquifer storage and recovery are excluded, along with the demands associated with them, because the question is whether the water reaching a place covers what people there drink from it.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed for 74 locations: 56 CalSim 3 demand units and 18 diversion nodes representing M&I deliveries that leave the model's geographic area. Each was confirmed to serve direct potable use by at least one community water system. Demand units designated in CalSim 3 as groundwater-only, and units whose deliveries go entirely to conjunctive use, are excluded.

Results are reported at the location level and do not correspond directly to any individual water system. To let a reader find results relevant to a particular community, the source documentation lists every California-regulated community water system drawing on each location — 475 systems in all, with a system appearing more than once where it draws on more than one location.

Locations are placed on a map using the service-area centroid of the largest system each serves by population, because some diversion nodes fall outside the model's geographic domain and have no spatial representation of their own.

What it is measured against

The reference is estimated recent potable demand for the specific surface water sources CalSim 3 models — not total demand, and not a projection. It is reconstructed from reported data rather than modeled, in three moves.

First, the surface water sources of California community water systems were catalogued and each source checked against the CalSim 3 report to decide whether the model represents it. Springs, local creeks, and independently operated reservoirs are outside the model; State Water Project and Central Valley Project contracts are inside it. Each represented source was assigned to a demand unit or diversion node. Purchased supplies were traced back to their originating sources, so that a system's demand is allocated across every location that ultimately serves it.

Second, those allocation shares were multiplied by each system's total potable demand — the volume of potable water used by residential and non-residential users in a calendar year, as reported to the state, with reporting periods spanning 2022 to 2025.

Third, system-level demands were summed to each of the 74 locations.

Where a system had not reported recent demand, the median demand for its hydrologic region was imputed. This affected 310 of 474 systems, and through them 63 of the 74 locations — a substantial share, and one of the principal limitations of the measure.

How the indicator is computed

Step 1 — annual deliveries per location

A crosswalk identifies the CalSim 3 delivery arcs carrying M&I surface water to each of the 74 locations; project carryover deliveries are included where applicable. Monthly deliveries are extracted for every scenario and aggregated to calendar years.

Step 2 — express each year as a share of demand

Each year's deliveries are divided by the location's estimated recent potable demand, giving a percentage of demand met for every year of every scenario.

Step 3 — count years at three levels

Three counts are taken across the 100 years: the share of years in which deliveries met at least 90% of demand — the definition of demand being met — and the shares meeting at least 70% and at least 50%. The 90% count is the reliability measure; the 70% and 50% figures describe how deep the shortfalls run in the years demand was not met.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

Each tier is a conjunction of a reliability condition and a severity condition, and the scenario takes the first tier whose conditions it satisfies in full.

Tier Reliability — years with at least 90% of demand met Severity — in the years demand was not met
Tier 1
Optimal		 at least 90% of years deliveries stay at or above 70% of demand in every such year
Tier 2
Acceptable		 at least 50% of years deliveries stay at or above 50% of demand in every such year — no critical shortfall occurs
Tier 3
At-risk		 at least 50% of years critical shortfalls — deliveries below 50% of demand — in no more than 20% of years
Tier 4
Critical		 none of the above

Tier 2 and Tier 3 share the same reliability condition. What separates them is severity alone: a location meeting demand in at least half of years falls to Tier 3 if even one year drops below half of demand, and to Tier 4 if more than a fifth of years do. A consequence worth knowing when reading results: a Tier 3 location can meet its demand more often than a Tier 2 location, and be in the lower tier because one of its short years was a critical shortfall.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For community water systems the continuous value is built from a drop risk — how close a location sits to falling into the next tier — rather than from a position along a single scale. It runs in three moves.

Rescale each criterion. Within the assigned tier, the reliability figure is rescaled to a 0-to-1 score against that tier's upper and lower reliability thresholds, and the severity figure against its upper and lower severity thresholds. In Tier 2, for example, the reliability score is the percentage of years demand was met, positioned between the Tier 2 threshold of 50% and the Tier 1 threshold of 90%.

Convert each score to a drop risk.

drop risk = (1 − score) × 0.99

so that a location scoring near the strong end of its tier has a drop risk near 0, and one near the weak end has a drop risk near 0.99.

Take the worse of the two, and add it to the tier. The location's continuous value is its tier number plus the higher of the reliability and severity drop risks — equivalently, the lower of the two rescaled scores. Whichever criterion is closer to failing governs, which matches how the tier itself is assigned.

Worked from the source document: a Tier 3 location meets demand in 78% of years, giving a rescaled reliability score of 0.70 and a reliability drop risk of 0.297; but deliveries fall below half of demand in 19% of years, against a Tier 3 allowance of 20%, giving a rescaled severity score of 0.059 and a severity drop risk of 0.93. The higher drop risk governs, so the continuous value is 3.93 — close to Tier 4, on the strength of severity, despite reliable deliveries in most years.

The 0.99 factor holds values strictly inside the tier's band, so the whole-number part always returns the discrete tier.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a community water systems tier

A tier value near 1 describes a location whose modeled surface deliveries cover its recent potable demand in almost every year, and never fall far short in the years they do not. Higher tier values describe locations where demand is met in fewer years, where the shortfalls run deeper, or both. Within a tier, a value near the top of the band means the location is close to falling into the next one, on whichever of reliability or severity is weaker.

What a community water systems tier does not resolve

Whether a community has enough water. This is the most important limit, and it is easy to misread. The measure covers only the surface water deliveries CalSim 3 models — principally State Water Project and Central Valley Project supplies. Groundwater, most local surface water, recycled water, and desalinated supply are all outside it, as are deliveries used for recharge or aquifer storage. A system with alternative supplies or long-term storage may absorb an unreliable surface source with no consequence for its customers; a system without them may not. The tier describes the reliability of a source, not the reliability of a provider, and emphatically not a provider's ability to serve its customers.

Any individual water system. Results are computed at the location level, which aggregates the systems drawing on a given delivery point. The 475 systems are listed against locations so a reader can find the relevant result, but no result is a result for one system.

Future demand. The reference is recent reported use, and for most systems it is imputed rather than reported. Recent use is an imperfect predictor of future need: losing a well raises reliance on surface water, and new wells or wellhead treatment lower it. CalSim 3 also holds demand static across each 100-year scenario.

Anything within a year. The measure is annual. Deliveries meeting annual demand can still arrive at the wrong time of year. Monthly deliveries are available separately in the data explorer.

Two additional measures, which produce no tiers

Because the reliability indicator above covers only modeled project deliveries, it cannot speak to shortage among drinking water providers overall, or to what such shortage costs. Two further measures address those gaps. Both are reported in the data explorer, both cover 83 locations — CalSim 3 demand units inside the model's Central Valley domain with M&I demand, whether surface, groundwater, or both — and neither is assigned a tier. They are documented here because these briefs are the project's only account of how its measures are constructed.

M&I supply shortage

This measure estimates the reduction in supply relative to total deliveries, surface and groundwater together, so that a cut in surface deliveries is weighed against the portfolio it falls on. For a provider drawing mostly on surface water a cut is consequential; for one drawing mostly on groundwater the same cut may be immaterial.

The method is the same one used for agricultural revenue, applied to municipal and industrial demand units. CalSim 3 assumes demands are met even in dry years by increasing groundwater pumping, so shortage does not appear in the model and must be inferred from the substitution. A year counts as a shortage year when surface deliveries fall below the 75th percentile benchmark of that location's surface deliveries and groundwater pumping exceeds its median — the second condition distinguishing a genuine shortfall from a wet year of lower demand. Shortage is expressed as ΔSW / (SW + GW). For scenarios other than the baseline, expected deliveries are taken from the baseline as surface deliveries plus shortages, and shortfalls are measured against that.

The measure treats every increase in groundwater pumping as evidence of shortage, which is a deliberately conservative reading: some of that pumping may be sustainable, particularly in basins not in long-term overdraft. Like the reliability indicator, it is computed at the demand unit scale and does not resolve local differences in groundwater access or infrastructure, and it does not represent demand that actually went unmet.

Welfare loss from supply shortage

This measure converts the modeled shortage into dollars: the amount residential consumers would have been willing to pay, above prevailing water rates, to avoid the reduction they face. It is reported as annual residential consumer welfare loss in dollars per year, at each of the 83 locations.

Prevailing volumetric tier prices and fixed monthly charges were compiled from utility rate schedules, available for 94 systems across 63 demand units, standardized to 2020 dollars and expressed per hundred cubic feet at a 12-ccf monthly residential billing benchmark, then weighted by service population to give a representative price per location. A constant price elasticity of residential water demand of ε = −0.4 is assumed, from California estimates. These calibrate a constant-elasticity inverse demand function, which is integrated from realized consumption up to full demand to give the annual welfare loss. Where there is no shortage the loss is zero. The function is convex, so each additional unit of water forgone commands a sharply rising willingness to pay — which is why modeled losses concentrate in a few dry years rather than spreading evenly.

Two limits beyond those inherited from the shortage measure. The estimate is consumer-side only and excludes producer surplus, so it is a consumer welfare loss rather than a total economic one. And it concerns quantity rather than quality: it does not capture willingness to pay for safer or better-tasting water, such as households buying bottled water.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to community water systems: the reliability indicator describes one source, not a water supply. It covers modeled project deliveries against the potable demand those deliveries currently serve, and a location's tier says nothing about whether the communities there have enough water, because it excludes every other supply they hold. The demand reference is reconstructed from reported use and imputed for most systems. The tier thresholds — 90%, 70%, and 50% of demand; 90%, 50%, and 20% of years — are chosen categories. And the two additional measures above produce no tiers and are not comparable with tier values from any sector.
