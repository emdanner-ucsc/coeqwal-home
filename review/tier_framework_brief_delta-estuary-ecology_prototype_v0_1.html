Tier framework brief

How Delta estuary ecology is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the Delta estuary ecology indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: Delta estuary ecology. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for Delta estuary ecology are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

The Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta was diked, drained, and reclaimed through the first half of the twentieth century, and more than ninety percent of its historical wetland and floodplain habitat was lost. What remains is a heavily altered estuary that nonetheless supports native and non-native species, including Delta smelt and Chinook salmon. How much fresh water flows through it shapes the conditions those species encounter.

The indicator starts from a single measured quantity: net Delta outflow, the volume of fresh water leaving the Delta after everyone drawing from the system upstream and inside it has taken their share. It is the closest available measure of how much fresh water remains available to the estuary itself.

Net Delta outflow is not used directly as the tier value. It is translated into an ecological index through four habitat conditions that respond to flow in known directions: the abundance of invasive submersed and floating aquatic vegetation, salinity, turbidity, and the availability of inundated microhabitat. Other conditions that matter to the Delta ecosystem — water temperature, land use, sea level rise, the abundance of invasive animals, food web structure — either do not respond to flow, or respond in ways too complex to represent here, and are outside the indicator entirely.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed at one location: Chipps Island, at the western edge of the Delta, where fresh water leaves the system for Suisun Bay. Net Delta outflow is a whole-system quantity, so a single measurement point represents the Delta as a whole rather than any particular part of it. Unlike sectors with many locations of interest, this sector produces one tier value per scenario.

What it is measured against

"High" and "low" outflow are defined relative to the modeled baseline, not to an observed record or an ecological target. Each year's average November-to-April outflow under current operations in the historical hydroclimate — the COEQWAL baseline scenario — is ranked, and the resulting distribution is cut into fifths. Those five bands are the outflow categories used for every scenario:

Two consequences follow from defining the categories this way, and both matter for reading the results. First, the baseline scenario contains twenty percent of its years in each category by construction — the bands were cut from its own distribution. A scenario's tier therefore describes how its winter outflow years are distributed relative to current operations under the historical hydroclimate. Second, the boundaries are volumes of water, not ecological thresholds; no ecological process is known to change at 54,000 cfs specifically.

Winter and spring flows are used because they set the conditions the estuary carries through the following summer.

How the indicator is computed

Each CalSim 3 scenario supplies 100 years of monthly net Delta outflow at Chipps Island. The computation runs in four steps.

Step 1 — one outflow value per year

Average net Delta outflow across November through April, giving 100 annual values, and assign each year to one of the five outflow categories above.

Step 2 — an ecological score for that year

Each outflow category carries a score from 1 to 5 for each of the four habitat conditions, assigned by the sector authors from what is known about how each condition responds to flow. A score of 1 marks the end of that condition's range the framework treats as the Tier 1 end; 5 marks the Tier 4 end. The four scores are summed, so a year's raw score runs from 4 to 20.

Note the direction on the first column: lower vegetation abundance scores toward Tier 1, because the vegetation in question is invasive — Brazilian waterweed and similar species, which clear the water, choke channels, and shelter non-native fish. Note also the shape of the first and fourth columns: both hold at 5 and 4 across four of the five categories and change only at extreme high flow. These are the two threshold responses in Figure 1 — the framework treats vegetation as being scoured out, and floodplain microhabitat as being inundated, only by the largest flows. Salinity and turbidity step evenly across all five categories.

Step 3 — smooth across three years

A three-year running mean of the raw scores gives each year a final score, still on the 4-to-20 range. This carries the effect of consecutive wet or dry periods, which the estuary experiences differently from the same years scattered apart.

Step 4 — rescale onto the tier range

Divide the final score by four.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

Dividing by four maps the 4-to-20 score range onto 1 to 5, which is the tier range directly. There are no separate cutoffs to apply: the score is the tier value once rescaled, and the whole-number part is the tier.

Because a year at extreme high outflow scores 4 and every other category scores 13 or more, the scale is not evenly exercised. A run of nothing but High-category years — the second of five categories — produces a final score of 13 and a value of 3.25, in Tier 3. Tier 1 and Tier 2 are reachable only through extreme high outflow years appearing repeatedly. This follows from the scoring matrix, where the invasive vegetation and microhabitat conditions change only at the top of the flow range.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For Delta estuary ecology the continuous value is not a position within the tier's interval. It summarizes the whole distribution of the 100 rescaled final scores:

continuous tier value = μ − 0.25 · SD

where μ is the mean of the rescaled final scores across the 100 years and SD is their standard deviation. The mean sets the position; the standard deviation adjusts it according to how widely conditions swing between years. A scenario whose winter outflow is steady year after year and one that alternates between extreme high and extreme low years can share a mean, and this term separates them.

Because the value is computed from the distribution rather than from a tier's boundaries, its whole-number part is not guaranteed by construction to match a separately assigned discrete tier, as it is in the sectors that interpolate within an interval.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a Delta estuary ecology tier

A tier value near 1 describes a Delta receiving extreme high winter outflow in most years, relative to the distribution under current operations in the historical hydroclimate: fresher water, more turbidity, inundated floodplain microhabitat, and invasive aquatic vegetation scoured back. Values through Tier 3 and Tier 4 describe a Delta receiving progressively less winter outflow, holding saltier and clearer water, with microhabitat inundated in fewer years and invasive vegetation persisting.

What a Delta estuary ecology tier does not resolve

The indicator is a function of one modeled quantity, net Delta outflow, at one point, averaged over one half of the year. Everything it reports about salinity, turbidity, vegetation, and microhabitat is inferred from outflow through the scoring matrix — none of the four is separately modeled, and none is measured. Conditions inside the Delta vary from place to place; this indicator does not describe any specific channel, marsh, or island.

The four habitat conditions are weighted equally in the sum, which is a choice in the construction of the index rather than a finding about their relative ecological importance. The scores in the matrix are expert assignments, not fitted values.

Most importantly, the range this indicator can move over is narrow relative to what determines the state of the Delta. The system's condition is set principally by things CalSim 3 does not vary: the wetlands and floodplains lost to reclamation, channelized waterways, sediment held behind dams, established invasive species, rising water temperatures, and sea level rise. Water management operates within those constraints. A tier value describes where a scenario sits along the flow-mediated part of the problem, which is the part the model can address.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to Delta estuary ecology: this indicator is a comparative index, not an ecological response model. It does not quantify habitat quality, species abundance, or ecosystem health, and a tier value cannot be read as a statement about how any species will fare. The outflow category boundaries are cut from the baseline scenario's own distribution, so tiers describe position relative to current operations rather than against any ecological standard. And because CalSim 3 carries built-in operating constraints, the scenarios represented are a subset of what could be modeled, let alone of what could occur.

Dev note — five items for the sector author, two of them substantive.
  1. The draft gives two different rules for assigning the discrete tier, and they are not the same procedure. The Methodology narrative assigns tiers from the three-year-mean score divided by four (the rule this brief documents). Table 3, headed "the discrete key outcome levels and their definitions," instead defines Tier 1 as "at least 65% of years receive extreme high average outflow compared to baseline," Tier 2 as 50–65%, Tier 3 as 25–50%, Tier 4 as none of the above. The two are related — extreme high years are the only ones scoring 4 — but they are not equivalent, and the 65 / 50 / 25 percentages are not derived anywhere in the document. Which is the operative rule? This brief follows the narrative and does not reproduce Table 3's percentages; the mapping table's fourth column paraphrases them as descriptions rather than as thresholds.
  2. The continuous formula and the stated intent disagree in sign. The text says the formula "penalis[es] higher variations as large swings between extreme conditions." The formula is μ − 0.25 SD, which lowers the value as variation rises — and lower is the Tier 1 direction. As written, wider swings move a scenario toward Tier 1, not away from it. There is a coherent ecological reading of that (occasional extreme high years are the only ones that scour vegetation and inundate microhabitat, so crediting variation is defensible), and there is the possibility of a sign error. This brief describes the term neutrally pending the author's answer. Whichever it is, the prose and the formula need to agree.
  3. An arithmetic slip and an out-of-range value. The draft reads: "a 'final score' of 20, the worst possible score, would become Level 4 (20 / 5 = 5, essentially 4.9999)." The divisor is 5 there and 4 everywhere else in the same passage. With the correct divisor, 20 / 4 = 5.00, which falls outside the stated 4.00–4.99 range, so a clipping rule must exist in the code and should be stated in the document. This brief's mapping table shows the Tier 4 band as 4.00–4.99 without asserting how 5.00 is handled.
  4. μ's scale is not stated. The continuous formula gives μ as "the mean of the distribution of the final scores," and final scores are defined on the 4-to-20 range — on which μ − 0.25 SD would return a value far outside 1–4.99. This brief assumes μ is the mean of the rescaled scores (after division by four). Worth one sentence in the source document.
  5. Table 4 is referenced but absent. The text points to "Table 4. Example calculations of final continuous outcome level from mean and standard deviations." The document contains three tables and three figures; there is no Table 4. Worked examples would resolve items 2 and 4 at a stroke, so this is worth chasing first.
