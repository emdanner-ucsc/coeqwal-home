Tier framework brief

How freshwater for in-Delta uses is measured and tiered

A technical reference brief describing the freshwater for in-Delta uses indicator: what it measures, where it is measured, how it is computed from CalSim 3 output, and how its values map onto the COEQWAL tier scale. This brief describes a measurement method; it reports no modeled tier results.

What this is

COEQWAL reports results for nine sectors of the California water system on a single four-level scale: Tier 1 (optimal), Tier 2 (acceptable), Tier 3 (at-risk), Tier 4 (critical). Lower tier numbers describe conditions closer to the Tier 1 end of each sector's scale. The nine sectors are measured in units that have nothing to do with one another — revenue, delivery reliability, river flow against an ecological target, salinity, storage, fish abundance — and the tier scale exists so that any of them can be held beside any other, and so that each can be compared with itself across scenarios.

A tier is a COEQWAL construction. Nothing in CalSim 3 produces one. Each sector has its own indicator, computed from model output by the project's subject-area experts for that sector, and its own rule for turning indicator values into tiers. The four tier labels are common to all nine sectors; the thresholds behind them are not, and are calibrated within each sector rather than across them. This brief documents the indicator and the thresholds for one sector: freshwater for in-Delta uses. There is a companion brief for each of the other eight.

This brief describes the measurement. It reports no results. Modeled tier values for freshwater for in-Delta uses are reported in the results briefs.

What the indicator measures

The Delta has been diked and managed for a century and a half to farm its islands and to move fresh water from upstream tributaries to diverters and pumps across the region. Because the Delta is an estuary, salt water pushes in from the west whenever fresh water flowing through it is insufficient to hold it back. Balancing inflows, in-Delta use, outflow, and export pumping so that Delta water stays fresh enough to drink and to irrigate with is a central part of how the system is operated.

The indicator measures how often water in the western Delta is fresh enough for those uses. It is built from salinity, expressed as electrical conductivity in microsiemens per centimetre, at two long-standing compliance stations, counted across every month of the 100-year simulation. It is a frequency measure: not how salty the Delta gets, but how much of the time it stays below each of three salinity levels.

Using all 1,200 monthly values, rather than an annual summary, is deliberate. It captures variation within years as well as between them. The months in which thresholds are exceeded will most often be late summer and fall, and drought periods, so the indicator largely reflects how reliably a scenario keeps fresh tributary inflow available during those critical stretches.

Where it is measured

The indicator is computed from two compliance stations in the western Delta and produces a single value per scenario.

StationRiverCalSim 3 variable
EmmatonSacramentoEM_EC_MONTH/SALINITY
Jersey PointSan JoaquinJP_EC_MONTH/SALINITY

Both are monthly mean values in the model's native units of µmhos/cm, equivalent to µS/cm. Salinity at these two points integrates the effects of tributary inflow and water use across much of the Delta, which is why they serve as indicators of conditions in Delta waterways generally, and why they appear in the observational, regulatory, and modeling record alike.

What it is measured against

The reference is a set of three fixed salinity thresholds, drawn from State Water Board benchmarks for drinking water quality and from published work on the agronomic and economic response of crops to salinity in irrigation water. Unlike most COEQWAL sectors, nothing here is referenced against a model run or an observed distribution: the thresholds are external and the same at both stations.

ThresholdValueWhat it marks
Low900 µS/cmthe boundary of recommended freshwater quality; below it, water is in the preferred range for all uses
Middle1,600 µS/cmthe upper limit of allowed long-term salinity; between this and the low threshold water remains usable, though salt-sensitive crops may have difficulty
High2,500 µS/cmno longer recommended for drinking water, and beginning to impair crop condition when used for irrigation

The framework treats small or infrequent excursions of higher salinity as manageable: a scenario is not moved to a higher tier by occasional exceedance. What determines the tier is whether salinity stays below the low or middle threshold most of the time, with a stated allowance for rarer exceedance of the high one.

How the indicator is computed

The computation runs over all 1,200 monthly values at both stations.

Step 1 — three fractions per station

At each station, count the share of the 1,200 months that fall on each side of the thresholds:

  • LT_A — the fraction of months below 900 µS/cm;
  • LT_B — the fraction of months below 1,600 µS/cm;
  • GT_C — the fraction of months above 2,500 µS/cm.

Step 2 — combine the two stations, taking the weaker one

The scenario's three metrics are the minimum of LT_A across the two stations, the minimum of LT_B, and the maximum of GT_C. Each metric is therefore taken from whichever station performed less well on it, which means the tier reflects the weaker of the two rather than an average. A scenario in which Emmaton stays fresh but Jersey Point does not is described by Jersey Point.

Step 3 — test the three metrics against the tier rules

The rules are evaluated in order, starting at Tier 1, and the scenario takes the first tier whose conditions it satisfies in full.

How indicator values map onto the tier scale

Each tier is a conjunction: all of its stated conditions must hold.

The step sizes are regular by design — 75%, 65%, 55% on the freshwater threshold; 5%, 12%, 20% on the high one — and were chosen as sensible increments from an examination of hydrologic patterns and historical salinity measurements rather than derived from any particular consequence. Tier 1 carries no middle-threshold condition, because a scenario below 900 µS/cm in three quarters of months is necessarily below 1,600 at least that often.

The continuous tier value

Alongside the whole-number tier, COEQWAL reports a continuous value that carries more resolution: 1.00–1.99 within Tier 1, 2.00–2.99 within Tier 2, 3.00–3.99 within Tier 3, and 4.00–4.99 within Tier 4. That banding is common to all nine sectors. How the value inside the band is constructed is not. Most sectors locate a scenario within its own tier's interval, so the whole-number part is the discrete tier by construction; others summarize a distribution, or measure a ratio against a reference, and carry no such guarantee. A continuous value should be read against the construction described below, not against another sector's.

For in-Delta uses the continuous value is built from the three metrics separately and then averaged. Each metric is placed on a 0-to-1 scale between the boundaries of the assigned tier: 0 at the boundary shared with the tier above, 1 at the boundary shared with the tier below. Because the metrics run in opposite directions — LT_A and LT_B move toward the Tier 1 end as they rise, GT_C as it falls — the interpolation is direction-aware, so that 0 always means the stronger end of the tier and 1 the weaker.

The three progress values are averaged into one, and that average is added to the tier number. Values are held strictly inside the tier's interval, so the whole-number part always returns the discrete tier.

The two boundary tiers need an anchor that the rules do not supply. Tier 1 is anchored at its stronger end by the endpoint of the scale — every month below 900 µS/cm, no month above 2,500 — and at its weaker end by the Tier 1 rule. Tier 4 is anchored at its stronger end by the Tier 3 rule and at its weaker end by the opposite endpoint: no month below either lower threshold, every month above the high one.

Continuous values are not read below one decimal place. COEQWAL treats a difference smaller than 0.1 as unstated: the project has not established whether such a difference is meaningful, and the briefs are built not to speak about it.

How to read a in-Delta salinity tier

A tier value near 1 describes a western Delta in which the freshest water — below 900 µS/cm — is present at least three quarters of the time, and water above 2,500 µS/cm appears in fewer than one month in twenty. Tiers 2 and 3 describe a Delta in which the freshest water is present less often and the saltiest more often. At Tier 4 water above 2,500 µS/cm is present more than a fifth of the time, or water below 900 µS/cm less than 55% of the time.

CalSim 3 scenarios representing current operating conditions under historical hydrology fall in Tier 2. Tier 1 therefore describes a Delta fresher than current conditions, and Tiers 3 and 4 describe it less fresh. Tier 2 itself describes a Delta in which water is predominantly fresh enough for both potable and irrigation use.

What an in-Delta salinity tier does not resolve

Where in the Delta. Emmaton and Jersey Point are useful because they are familiar and long-established, but Delta salinity varies in space and time, and these two points do not always represent conditions relevant to every in-Delta use. South Delta channels in particular can become saltier than the western stations indicate, depending on San Joaquin inflows, export pumping, and antecedent hydrology. The indicator was built to respond to the large-scale factors that move Delta salinity — flows, regulations, pumping, in-Delta consumptive use, tides, sea level rise — and it is a starting point for examining local conditions rather than a substitute for doing so.

When. Every month of every year carries equal weight. Salinity in a month when no one is irrigating counts the same as salinity at the height of the growing season. The tier definitions could be rebuilt around specific end uses and timings if that were the object; they were deliberately not, in favor of a measure that generalizes.

What follows from a tier. The frequencies are not calibrated to salinity-related consequences. A Tier 3 result does not indicate or guarantee that any particular change in crop choice, irrigation practice, or drinking water treatment would occur.

What this doesn't say

This brief reports modeled results from a specific ensemble. It does not predict what will happen, evaluate whether those results are acceptable, or recommend any course of action. The tier framework provides definitional categories; the meaning of those categories for any individual reader, community, or decision is not specified by COEQWAL.

Specific to in-Delta uses: the tier boundaries are regular increments chosen for their reasonableness against hydrologic and historical patterns, not derived from measured consequences, and different increments would redistribute the tiers without any modeled salinity changing. Salinity in CalSim 3 is itself estimated — by a neural network emulating the DSM2 hydrodynamic model — rather than simulated directly. And because the two stations are combined by taking the weaker of the two on each metric, a tier describes the less fresh of two points in the western Delta, not the Delta as a whole.
